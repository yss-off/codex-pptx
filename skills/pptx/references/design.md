# Presentation Design Reference

Use this file to choose a visual direction. Adapt the guidance to the audience, content density, template, and display environment.

## Start with intent

Choose a dominant visual idea connected to the subject: a restrained technical grid, documentary evidence, editorial typography, a material texture, or another content-informed motif. Keep the motif consistent without forcing identical layouts.

Use one dominant color, supporting neutrals, and one or two accents. Validate contrast after rendering. Palette examples are starting points, not mandatory themes:

## Density and shape language

| Style | Typical margin | Typical gap | Corner treatment | Best fit |
|-------|----------------|-------------|------------------|----------|
| Compact | 0.3–0.4 in | 0.1–0.25 in | square or slight | dense technical tables |
| Balanced | 0.4–0.5 in | 0.2–0.4 in | modest rounding | general business decks |
| Spacious | 0.5–0.6 in | 0.3–0.6 in | rounded | product and narrative decks |
| Airy | 0.6 in or more | 0.4–0.8 in | pill used sparingly | brand-led or launch decks |

Do not apply arbitrary radii to every component. Outer containers should not look sharper than nested pill-shaped elements unless intentional.

## Typography

- Detect fonts on the rendering host before selecting them.
- Use one CJK-safe family consistently for Chinese unless the design intentionally pairs compatible families.
- Prefer roughly 28–44 pt for ordinary slide titles, 14–20 pt for body text, and 10–12 pt for sources, then adjust for room size and content density.
- Reserve bold for hierarchy and emphasis; do not make all body copy bold.
- Check line breaks, punctuation, Latin/CJK baseline alignment, and optical centering after rendering.

## Layout patterns

Choose patterns by content relationship:

- Evidence plus interpretation: chart or screenshot with one clear takeaway.
- Comparison: aligned columns with identical measures.
- Process: numbered stages with direction and handoff points.
- System explanation: block diagram with boundaries and proof points.
- Decision: alternatives, trade-offs, recommendation, and requested action.
- Image-led narrative: one dominant image with restrained annotation.
- Dense reference: compact table or appendix page with explicit reading order.

Avoid decorative visuals that compete with the claim. A deliberately sparse slide can be better than an irrelevant icon grid.

## Data and evidence

- State what a chart proves, not merely what it contains.
- Include units, denominator, time range, experimental condition, and source when relevant.
- Use a consistent encoding for the same variable across slides.
- Distinguish measured results, simulations, estimates, and targets visually and verbally.
- Do not imply final-platform proof from a simulation or demonstration platform.
